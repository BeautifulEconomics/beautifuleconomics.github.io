## The claim

A degree from a good university takes four years and a lot of money. For many jobs, almost nothing you learn in those four years is used on day one. Yet employers pay graduates more. Why?

One answer: university taught them useful things. The other answer is more unsettling. University did not need to teach them anything. It just needed to be hard enough that only certain people could finish it. The degree is not proof of learning. It is proof of ability. The diploma does not say "I know things." It says "I am the kind of person who can get through this."

Michael Spence proposed this idea in 1973. It won him a Nobel Prize. His insight: when one side of a deal knows something the other does not, the informed side can take a costly action to prove what they know. A worker knows their own ability, but the employer does not. So the worker goes to university. The key is that the action must cost more for the people you want to filter out. If university were equally easy for everyone, it would prove nothing.

## How it works

A signal needs three things to function.

**It must be costly.** If anyone could send it cheaply, everyone would, and it would carry no information. A peacock's tail is a signal of genetic fitness. It works because growing and carrying that enormous tail is expensive. A weak bird with a big tail would be eaten. The cost is the proof.

**The cost must be different for different types.** A talented worker finds university hard but doable. A less talented worker finds it nearly impossible. This gap in cost is what separates the two groups. If it costs the same for everyone, it separates no one.

**The other side cannot check directly.** If employers could test ability on day one with perfect accuracy, nobody would need a degree. Signalling exists because there is an information gap.

## Where you see it

**Education.** Bryan Caplan's *The Case Against Education* (2018) makes the case that about 80% of the extra pay from a college degree comes from signalling, not from what you actually learned. His best evidence is the "sheepskin effect." A student who does three years and drops out earns far less than a student who finishes the fourth year. But the fourth year barely adds more knowledge. What it adds is the diploma. That piece of paper is the signal.

**Luxury goods.** Thorstein Veblen noticed in 1899 that the rich spend money in ways that look wasteful, not in spite of the waste but because of it. A Rolex keeps time no better than a ₹500 watch. The price *is* the message. In India, a lavish wedding signals family standing not by what it gives the couple but by what it costs the family. The expense is the proof.

**Warranties.** When a company offers a five-year warranty, it is not just promising repairs. It is telling you it expects very few things to break. A company selling a fragile product cannot afford to make that promise. The offer itself is the information.

**Religion.** Biologist Amotz Zahavi and economist Laurence Iannaccone (1992) both argued that costly religious practices like fasting, pilgrimage, and tithing serve as signals of commitment. The cost keeps out people who are not serious. Only the truly committed will bear the expense.

**Unpaid internships.** Working for free in an expensive city signals not just willingness but family money. The information sent is partly about talent and partly about resources. This is why signalling can increase inequality even as it solves an information problem.

## Where it stops working

Not all signals get read correctly. Some are faked long enough to fool the receiver. Some become so common they stop separating anyone. When nearly everyone has a bachelor's degree, it stops being a useful signal, and the market starts demanding a master's degree. This is called credential inflation. The signal degrades, and the cost shifts upward.

There is also a real debate about how much of education is signalling versus real skill-building. Spence himself said both happen at the same time. The honest answer is that most economists think signalling accounts for somewhere between 30% and 80% of the value of a degree. That is a wide range, and it tells you how much is still unsettled.

## The one thing to keep

When you see someone doing something that looks irrational or wasteful, ask: who is watching, and what does the cost prove? Often the waste is the entire point.

### Sources and further reading

- Spence, M. (1973). "Job Market Signaling." *Quarterly Journal of Economics*, 87(3), 355–374. The paper that started it all.
- Caplan, B. (2018). *The Case Against Education.* Princeton University Press. The strongest modern case for the signalling view.

- Connelly, B.L. et al. (2011). "Signaling Theory: A Review and Assessment." *Journal of Management*, 37(1), 39–67. A good survey across fields.

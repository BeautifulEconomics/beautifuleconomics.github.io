## The claim

A hospital is told its funding depends on how quickly it treats patients in the emergency room. Within months, waiting times fall. The newspapers celebrate. But the doctors know what actually happened — the hospital started reclassifying patients so the difficult cases no longer counted as emergency admissions. The clock looks better. The patients are not better off.

This is Goodhart's law. Charles Goodhart, a British economist advising the Bank of England, noticed it first with money supply targets in 1975. The Bank would pick a monetary aggregate to control — say M3, the broad money supply — and as soon as markets knew the target, they found ways to move money that technically fell outside M3 while achieving the same economic effect. The measure stopped telling the Bank what it used to tell them.

Goodhart put it plainly: "Any observed statistical regularity will tend to collapse once pressure is placed upon it for control purposes." The anthropologist Marilyn Strathern later gave it its sharpest form: when a measure becomes a target, it ceases to be a good measure.

## Why it happens

A good measure works because people are not trying to manipulate it. The moment you attach a reward or punishment to that number, you have changed the incentives of everyone the number describes. They will optimise for the number. Not always for what the number was supposed to represent.

This is not necessarily dishonest. Teachers whose school funding depends on standardised test scores are not villains for spending more time on test preparation and less on curiosity. They are responding rationally to the system they have been placed in. The problem is in the design, not the person.

The mechanism underneath is a principal-agent problem. The principal (government, manager, regulator) cannot observe the thing they actually care about — quality of education, genuine health outcomes, real financial stability — so they pick a proxy they *can* observe. The agent (teacher, doctor, bank) then has every reason to maximise the proxy, especially where the proxy and the real thing diverge.

## Where you see it

**Education.** When No Child Left Behind tied US school funding to standardised test scores, schools in multiple states narrowed their curricula dramatically. Brian Jacob and Steven Levitt (2003) documented that some Chicago teachers changed answers on their students' test sheets. The scores improved. Learning, on average, did not.

**Policing.** When New York's CompStat system rewarded precincts for reducing reported crime, officers reclassified felonies as misdemeanours. A robbery became "lost property." John Eterno and Eli Silverman documented this systematically in their 2012 study of retired NYPD officers — the pressure to hit numbers changed what got recorded, not what happened on the street.

**India's colonial cobra bounty.** The British government in Delhi offered a bounty for dead cobras to reduce the cobra population. People started breeding cobras for the reward. When the government scrapped the programme, breeders released their now-worthless cobras, and the population increased. This is sometimes called the cobra effect, but it is Goodhart's law in its purest form — the bounty measured dead cobras, not fewer cobras.

**Academia.** When universities started ranking researchers by publication count, the number of published papers worldwide exploded. So did the number of journals willing to publish anything for a fee. Campbell's law — the sociologist Donald Campbell's independent and slightly earlier formulation of the same idea — predicted this: "The more any quantitative social indicator is used for social decision-making, the more subject to corruption pressures it will be."

## Where it stops working as an explanation

Goodhart's law is not an argument against measurement. It is an argument against naive measurement. Some targets work well — road speed limits reduce fatalities even though everyone knows the target. The question is always how easy the proxy is to game relative to genuinely improving the underlying thing.

Not every failed policy is Goodhart's law either. Sometimes the measure was simply wrong from the start, not corrupted by being targeted. And sometimes people game a target yet outcomes still improve, because optimising for the proxy does partly improve the real thing too. The law describes a tendency, not a certainty.

## The one thing to keep

Next time you see a number improving — test scores up, crime stats down, quarterly targets hit — ask a second question: did the thing the number is supposed to measure also improve, or did the number just learn to take care of itself?

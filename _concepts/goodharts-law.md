## The claim

A hospital is told its funding depends on how quickly it treats emergency patients. Within months, waiting times fall. The newspapers celebrate. But the doctors know what really happened. The hospital started reclassifying patients so the hard cases no longer counted as emergencies. The clock looks better. The patients are not better off.

This is Goodhart's law. Charles Goodhart was a British economist who advised the Bank of England. In 1975, he noticed something strange. The Bank would pick a number to control, like the broad money supply. But as soon as markets knew the target, they found ways around it. They moved money through channels that technically did not count. The number stopped meaning what it used to mean.

Goodhart said it simply: any number that works well as a measure will stop working the moment you use it as a target. The anthropologist Marilyn Strathern made it even shorter: when a measure becomes a target, it ceases to be a good measure.

## Why it happens

A good measure works because nobody is trying to game it. The moment you attach a reward or a punishment to a number, you change the behaviour of everyone that number describes. They will try to improve the number. Not always the thing the number was supposed to track.

This is not dishonesty. Teachers whose school funding depends on test scores are not bad people for spending more time on test prep and less on real learning. They are doing what the system rewards. The problem is in the design, not the person.

Here is the pattern. A government, a manager, or a regulator wants to improve something they cannot directly see, like the quality of education or the health of an economy. So they pick a number they *can* see and use it as a stand-in. But the people being measured have every reason to make the number look good, especially when that is easier than actually improving the real thing.

## Where you see it

**Education.** The US law No Child Left Behind tied school funding to test scores. Schools across multiple states narrowed what they taught to focus on the test. Brian Jacob and Steven Levitt (2003) found that some Chicago teachers went further and changed answers on their students' test sheets. The scores went up. Actual learning, on average, did not.

**Policing.** New York's CompStat system rewarded police precincts for reducing crime numbers. So officers started recording robberies as "lost property" and downgrading felonies to lesser offences. John Eterno and Eli Silverman (2012) surveyed retired NYPD officers and confirmed this was widespread. What changed was the record, not the street.

**Colonial India.** The British government in Delhi wanted fewer cobras. They offered money for every dead cobra brought in. It worked at first. Then people started breeding cobras just to kill them and collect the reward. When the government found out and stopped the programme, the breeders released their now-worthless snakes. Delhi ended up with more cobras than before.

**Academia.** When universities started judging researchers by how many papers they published, the number of papers exploded. So did the number of low-quality journals happy to publish anything for a fee. The sociologist Donald Campbell predicted this independently. His version: the more a number is used for decisions, the more people will find ways to corrupt it.

## Where it stops working as an explanation

Goodhart's law is not an argument against measuring things. It is an argument against measuring things badly. Some targets work fine. Speed limits reduce road deaths even though every driver knows the number. The real question is always: how easy is it to game this number compared to genuinely improving the thing it measures?

Also, not every failed policy is Goodhart's law. Sometimes the measure was just wrong from the start. And sometimes people game a target but outcomes still get a bit better, because improving the number partly improves the real thing too. The law describes a pattern, not a guarantee.

## The one thing to keep

Next time you see a number improving, ask a second question. Did the thing the number is supposed to measure also improve? Or did the number just learn to take care of itself?

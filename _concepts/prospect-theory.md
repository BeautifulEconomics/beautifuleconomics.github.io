## The claim

Standard economics says a rational person looks at the final result. If you have ₹1,00,000 and someone offers you a coin flip that could leave you with ₹1,50,000 or ₹80,000, you should calmly calculate which final amount you prefer and decide.

Daniel Kahneman and Amos Tversky ran experiments in the 1970s and found that almost nobody thinks this way. People do not care about where they end up. They care about the *change* from where they are now. And they feel gains and losses very differently.

Losing ₹100 hurts about twice as much as gaining ₹100 feels good. The pain of losing is not just the flip side of the joy of winning. It is bigger, sharper, and more urgent. This is called loss aversion, and it is the central finding of prospect theory, published in 1979. That paper became the most cited paper in all of economics.

## How it works

Prospect theory changes two things about how economists model decisions.

**People judge changes, not totals.** You do not look at your total wealth and feel happy or sad. You look at what changed since yesterday, since last month, since what you expected. The starting point matters enormously. Going from ₹10,000 to ₹11,000 feels great. Going from ₹15,000 to ₹11,000 feels terrible. Same final number, completely different experience.

There is also a pattern in how changes feel. The jump from ₹100 to ₹200 feels bigger than the jump from ₹1,000 to ₹1,100, even though both are ₹100. Each extra rupee matters a little less as the amounts grow. This works the same way for losses.

**People get probabilities wrong in a specific way.** We overweight tiny chances and underweight large ones. This is why the same person can buy a lottery ticket (overweighting the tiny chance of winning big) and also buy insurance (overweighting the tiny chance of disaster). In standard economics, doing both at once makes no sense. In prospect theory, it is the same mental habit at work.

Kahneman and Tversky estimated that losses feel roughly 2 to 2.5 times as strong as equal-sized gains. Later studies found the number varies from about 1.5 to 3, depending on the situation. The exact number is not fixed, but the pattern — losses hit harder — is one of the most replicated findings in all of behavioural science.

## Where you see it

**The mug experiment.** Richard Thaler noticed in 1980 that people ask for much more money to give up something they own than they would pay to buy the same thing. Kahneman, Knetsch, and Thaler (1990) tested this with coffee mugs. Students who were given a mug wanted about twice as much to sell it as other students were willing to pay for it. Owning the mug changed the starting point. Selling it now felt like a loss.

**Stock markets.** Terrance Odean (1998) studied 10,000 real brokerage accounts and found something striking. Investors were far more likely to sell stocks that had gone up than stocks that had gone down. Selling a losing stock means admitting the loss is real, which is painful. So people hold their losers too long and sell their winners too early. This is the exact opposite of smart tax strategy.

**Insurance.** People pay insurance premiums far above what the risk is actually worth. Sydnor (2010) found that homeowners chose insurance plans implying they valued avoiding a ₹1,000 loss at ₹3,000 to ₹5,000. They were not bad at maths. They were paying to avoid the feeling of loss.

**Pay cuts.** A salary drop from ₹50,000 to ₹45,000 feels much worse than a raise from ₹45,000 to ₹50,000 feels good, even though the change is the same ₹5,000. This is why companies almost never cut wages directly. They would rather freeze pay and let inflation do the cutting quietly. Bewley (1999) confirmed this by interviewing over 300 business managers.

**Everyday decisions.** Why will someone drive across town to save ₹200 on a ₹500 purchase but not to save ₹200 on a ₹50,000 purchase? The saving is the same. But ₹200 feels like a bigger deal against a smaller starting number. Why do restaurants offer money-back guarantees? Because removing the chance of loss changes how the whole decision feels.

## Where it stops working

Prospect theory describes how people actually make decisions. It does not say this is how they *should* decide. For long-term investing, retirement planning, or any repeated decision, you would often be better off ignoring your gut feeling about losses and thinking in expected values instead. The theory is a description, not advice.

The theory also has a weak spot: the starting point. Where exactly is the line between "gain" and "loss"? Kőszegi and Rabin (2006) suggested the starting point is not where you are now but what you expected to happen. This helps in some cases but makes the theory harder to pin down.

Some recent studies have also questioned whether loss aversion is as strong as 2x in every situation. Yechiam (2019) reviewed the evidence and found it depends on the context. For small amounts or experienced traders, the gap may be smaller. The core idea holds, but the details are still being worked out.

## The one thing to keep

You do not experience life in totals. You experience it in changes — from what you had, what you expected, what you thought was yours. And the drops register louder than the rises. Every decision you make is shaped by this, whether you notice it or not.

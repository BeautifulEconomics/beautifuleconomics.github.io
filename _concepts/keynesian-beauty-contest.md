## The claim

In 1936, John Maynard Keynes described a newspaper contest from his time. Readers were shown photographs of faces and asked to pick the most attractive one. But here is the twist: the winner was not the person who picked the best-looking face. The winner was the person who picked the face that *most other people* picked.

Think about what this does to your thinking. You do not pick the face you find prettiest. You pick the face you think the average person will find prettiest. But if everyone is doing this, you should actually pick the face you think the average person thinks the average person will find prettiest. It keeps going. Each step takes you further from anyone's real opinion.

Keynes used this to explain stock markets. The price of a stock, he said, does not reflect what a company is actually worth. It reflects what investors think other investors will pay for it. Professional investing "is not a game of picking the prettiest face but of guessing which face the other players will pick."

## How it works

In a standard economics textbook, prices are supposed to settle at the true value. A company worth ₹100 should trade near ₹100, because smart investors will buy it when it is cheap and sell when it is expensive, pushing the price toward truth.

Keynes saw a different reality. If enough people in the market are playing the guessing game — trying to predict what others will do rather than figuring out real value — prices can stay far from the truth for a long time. You can be perfectly smart and still buy an overpriced stock, if you believe others will push it even higher before you sell. You are not being foolish. You are playing a different game.

The experimental economist Rosemarie Nagel tested this in 1995 with a simple game. Everyone picks a number between 0 and 100. The winner is whoever is closest to two-thirds of the group's average. If everyone picks 50, two-thirds is about 33. But if everyone figures that out and picks 33, two-thirds is 22. And so on, all the way down toward zero. In experiments, most people only think one or two steps ahead. They do not go all the way. This finding opened up a whole area of research on how people actually think in strategic situations.

## Where you see it

**Stock market bubbles.** During the dot-com bubble of the late 1990s, many professional investors knew that companies with no revenue were wildly overpriced. They bought anyway, because they expected others to keep buying. Chuck Prince, the CEO of Citigroup, said it perfectly in 2007, just before the financial crisis: "As long as the music is playing, you've got to get up and dance." He knew the music would stop. He just thought he could sit down in time.

**Social media.** A creator does not post what they think is their best work. They post what they think the algorithm will push. The algorithm is trained on what other users click on. So creators are guessing what other people like, based on a system that guesses what other people like. The result is a feed shaped by everyone's guess about everyone else, which is nobody's actual taste.

**Fashion.** Nobody in a trend is necessarily wearing what they personally like. They are wearing what they think will be seen as current. And what counts as current depends on what others are wearing. The sociologist Georg Simmel described this loop in 1904, long before anyone had the language of game theory.

**Cryptocurrency.** A meme coin has no earnings, no assets, no products. Its price is entirely about what buyers think other buyers will do next. This is not a judgment call. It is a description of the game's structure. The beauty contest in its purest form.

**Elections.** When voters think about "electability" — not who they prefer, but who they think others will vote for — they are playing the beauty contest. A candidate can lose support not because people stopped liking them, but because people stopped believing others liked them.

## Where it stops working

The beauty contest is not the only force in markets. Long-term investors, value investors, and people with better information do push prices toward reality, especially over time. The beauty contest matters most in the short run, in markets with many casual participants, and when nobody agrees on what the real value is.

Keynes was not saying everyone is foolish. His point is more interesting than that. Individually smart behaviour — correctly guessing what the crowd will do — can add up to collectively strange outcomes, where prices drift away from anything real. The problem is not the players. It is the game.

## The one thing to keep

When a price, a trend, or a viral post makes no sense to you, consider that it may not make sense to anyone involved. Everyone might be guessing what everyone else thinks, and no one might be thinking about the thing itself.

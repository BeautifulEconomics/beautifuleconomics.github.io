## The claim

During British rule in India, Delhi had too many cobras. The government offered money for every dead cobra brought in. At first it worked. People killed cobras and collected the reward. Then some people started breeding cobras just to kill them and claim the money. When the government found out and ended the programme, the breeders released their now-worthless snakes into the streets. Delhi ended up with more cobras than before.

The economist Horst Siebert named this the cobra effect: when a solution to a problem actually makes the problem worse, because the reward it created pointed in the wrong direction.

The logic is simple. The government wanted fewer live cobras. It paid for dead cobras. But these are not the same thing. Once a dead cobra had a price, a live cobra became something worth producing. The policy accidentally created a business it never intended.

## Why it happens

Every incentive carries a hidden assumption about how people will react. The cobra bounty assumed that dead cobras could only come from the wild population. It did not consider that people might *create* the thing they were being paid to destroy.

This connects to a bigger idea in economics. In 1976, Robert Lucas argued that people change their behaviour when policy changes. Any policy that assumes people will keep acting the same way is at risk. The cobra effect is the extreme version: people do not just adjust a little. They reverse the whole thing.

## Where you see it

**Hanoi's rat bounty.** In 1902, the French colonial government in Hanoi had a rat problem in the sewers. They offered money for every rat tail brought in. People started cutting tails off rats and letting them go, since a rat without a tail breeds just fine. Some people started rat farms. Historian Michael Vann documented this in 2003. The rat population grew.

**Protecting endangered species.** In the US, the Endangered Species Act restricts what landowners can do with land where endangered species are found. Dean Lueck and Jeffrey Michael (2003) found that some landowners responded by clearing their land before any endangered animal could be spotted on it. The law meant to protect habitat gave people a reason to destroy it faster.

**The Streisand effect.** In 2003, Barbra Streisand sued a photographer to remove an aerial photo of her house from a public database. Before the lawsuit, the photo had been downloaded six times. After the lawsuit made headlines, it was downloaded over 420,000 times. The attempt to hide something created the audience for it.

**Carbon offsets.** Some early carbon credit schemes paid factories in developing countries to destroy a greenhouse gas called HFC-23. Michael Wara (2007) found that some factories started producing more of the pollutant on purpose, just so they could earn more credits for destroying it. The clean-up scheme became a reason to pollute more.

**Fossil destruction in China.** Farmers in some parts of China learned that reporting fossils on their land leads to restrictions or land seizure. So many fossils are quietly destroyed before anyone official can see them. The law designed to protect fossils gives people a reason to smash them.

## Where it stops working as an explanation

Not every bad outcome is a cobra effect. Sometimes a policy just does nothing. The cobra effect specifically means the policy made the problem *worse* by creating a new behaviour that backfired.

Also, the original cobra story itself might be partly legend. Siebert told it without a primary historical source, and historians have not confirmed every detail. The Hanoi rat story, on the other hand, is well documented. The concept is solid even if the founding story is more parable than precise history.

And the cobra effect is not a reason to never use incentives. It is a reason to think one step further before designing them. Ask: what new behaviour does this reward make profitable? Who could game it? Policies that survive those questions tend to work.

## The one thing to keep

Before you pay someone to solve a problem, ask: could they earn more by creating the problem first?

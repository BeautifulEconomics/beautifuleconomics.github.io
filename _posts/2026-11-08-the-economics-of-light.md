Nobody lights a diya for the lumens. The phone in your pocket throws a brighter beam than a hundred oil lamps. If illumination were the point, Diwali would have been replaced by a sale on LED bulbs long ago.

Yet tonight, across balconies, doorsteps and temple courtyards, people will set out small clay lamps filled with oil and a cotton wick. They will do this knowing the lamps are faint, knowing they will burn out by morning, knowing it would be simpler not to. And they will do it because everyone else is doing it too.

## A coordination game on every doorstep

Economists study situations where the value of what you do depends on what everyone else does. These are called coordination games, and they are quietly some of the hardest problems in economics.

Think about it from one household's point of view. A single diya on a dark street is barely visible. A hundred diyas on the same street are beautiful. Your lamp is not worth much on its own. It is worth a great deal if your neighbours light theirs.

This is the deep structure of Diwali's light. It is a public good — not in the loose, everyday sense, but in the precise economic sense. Your diya adds to the beauty of the street without taking anything from anyone else's experience. It is non-rival and non-excludable, the two properties that make public goods so difficult to provide through markets.

## The problem that should exist but does not

Here is what is interesting. Public goods are supposed to be underprovided. That is one of the most reliable results in economics. If everyone benefits from the lit street whether or not they personally bother to light a diya, the rational move is to let the neighbours do it. Free-riding is individually optimal. The street should stay dark.

But the street does not stay dark. Diwali solves the free-rider problem, and it does so without taxes, subsidies, regulation, or even a committee. It uses something older: a shared story.

The story says: this is the night of light. The story says: your grandmother did this, and her grandmother before her. The story says: everyone lights tonight. And because everyone believes the story, everyone acts on it, and because everyone acts on it, the story stays true.

Economists call this a focal point — a shared expectation that coordinates behaviour without explicit agreement. Thomas Schelling described the idea in 1960, but cultures have been engineering focal points for millennia. A festival is a focal point that repeats.

## What markets cannot do

Markets are extraordinary at solving allocation problems. They move goods to whoever values them most, they signal scarcity through prices, they coordinate strangers across continents. But they are not very good at making a street beautiful on the same night every year.

For that you need a shared calendar, a shared ritual, and a shared willingness to do something that is privately costless but collectively transformative. You need culture.

This is not a sentimental point. It is a structural one. There are coordination problems that price mechanisms cannot reach — problems where the value is created by simultaneous participation, where the thing being produced is indivisible and where the decision to contribute is binary. Light or do not light. Show up or stay home.

Festivals solve these problems. They create common knowledge: not just "I will light a lamp" but "I know that you know that everyone will light a lamp tonight." And common knowledge, as any game theorist will tell you, is the ingredient that turns a coordination problem into a coordination success.

## The lamp that is not about light

So tonight, if you light a diya, you are doing something more interesting than following tradition. You are participating in a solution to a collective-action problem that predates economics as a discipline by several thousand years.

The lamp is not about the light it gives. It is about the light it gives *when placed next to everyone else's*.

Happy Diwali.
